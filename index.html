DeepPace

Forecasting hotel demand from the 2D booking-pace surface

Why treating booking history as a 2D surface over (arrival_date, DTA) — instead of collapsing it into a 1D time series — is the right way to think about hotel demand forecasting, and why we built a synthetic benchmark to test that claim.

Motivation Challenges References

Motivation

Hotel demand forecasting is usually built on a 1D series of daily totals, with pace expressed as a handful of hand-engineered features (rooms on the books at 7/30/90 days out, pickup between two DTA points, and so on). DeepPace-Sim starts from a different premise: the real object being modelled is OTB(property, arrival_date, DTA) — net rooms on the books DTA days before an arrival date — a genuinely two-dimensional surface, not a 1D series with extra columns bolted on.

The two axes carry different physics

Moving along arrival_date traverses seasonality and demand level. Moving along DTA traverses pace — how a fixed date fills over time. A model handed a flattened feature vector has to rediscover that split from scratch; a model handed the surface gets it as geometry.

The same on-books number can mean opposite things

At DTA = 120, a date that will sell out and a date that will finish at 40% occupancy can both sit at the same low room count. What separates them is context — day of week, season, events, and the pace of neighbouring dates. Any model that maps OTB(d, k) → rooms_sold(d) without that context is guessing. This is not a hypothetical: it's a direct consequence of compression — high-demand dates shift every booking-segment's arrival distribution earlier, so peak and trough dates converge at long lead times and only diverge close to arrival.

Calendar effects are diagonal

Since booking_date = arrival_date − DTA, a flash sale on one booking date touches every arrival date at a different DTA — it shows up as a diagonal streak in (arrival_date, DTA) space, not a vertical or horizontal one. The same is true of the booking-day-of-week ripple: reservations are made mostly on weekdays, imprinting a 7-day oscillation along the DTA axis whose phase depends on the arrival date's own day of week. Features computed independently per (arrival_date, DTA) cell cannot see either effect.

Why simulate instead of using real data

Real property-management-system extracts can tell you a model won or lost, but never why. DeepPace-Sim generates data bottom-up from individual reservations — a booking date, a party size, a cancellation outcome — with capacity enforced by rejecting arrivals in chronological order. Every latent that produced the data (true demand intensity, true segment mixture, true arrival-time distribution, turn-aways, the promo calendar) is persisted alongside it. That turns the dataset into a benchmark: a model's recovered structure can be checked against ground truth, not just its point forecasts.

General challenges of forecasting with booking-pace information

Right-censoring on sold-out dates
Observed rooms_sold understates true demand whenever a date sells out — capacity rejects arrivals, it doesn't record them. A plain regression loss systematically under-forecasts exactly the peak dates that matter most operationally.
Segment mixture varies by context, not just by property
The same property books differently on a Tuesday in February than a Saturday in July, because the mix of group / leisure / last-minute reservations shifts with day-of-week, season, and events — not because some fixed "shape" changed. Forecasters that fit one global booking curve per property miss this.
Compression collapses information at long lead times
The pace curves of very different outcomes look nearly identical far from arrival and only separate close-in. A model needs context (season, DOW, events, capacity pressure) to break that ambiguity — the raw on-books number alone is not enough.
Calendar effects are diagonal, not axis-aligned
Promotions and booking-day-of-week patterns live on the booking_date axis, which cuts diagonally through (arrival_date, DTA) space. Feature pipelines that treat each arrival date independently cannot see them.
Leakage across the as-of boundary is easy to introduce by accident
A feature for as-of date t may only use booking activity with booking_date ≤ t. Because the same DTA corresponds to a different booking_date for every arrival date, a single fixed DTA cutoff applied across a window of arrival dates leaks future information.
Non-stationarity: trend, shocks, and slow demand drift
Occupancy trends up or down over multi-year horizons (recovery ramps, organic growth), and individual properties carry persistent runs of over- or under-performance that look like noise on any single day but are structured over weeks.
No ground truth in real PMS data
Real extracts can score a model's accuracy but can't confirm why it won — whether it actually recovered the underlying demand structure, or got the right answer for the wrong reason. That's only checkable against a simulator whose latents are known.

References

Booking-curve & pickup forecasting (closest prior art)

  • Weatherford, L.R. & Kimes, S.E. (2003). "A comparison of forecasting methods for hotel revenue management." International Journal of Forecasting. The classic hotel pickup / booking-curve forecasting comparison — the field's standard term for what this project calls booking pace is "pickup" over the "booking window."
  • "The Booking Window Evolution and its Impact on Hotel Revenue Management Forecasting" (2018).
  • "Hotel revenue management forecasting accuracy: the hidden impact of booking windows" (2021), Journal of Hospitality and Tourism Insights.

Real-world analog dataset

  • Antonio, N., de Almeida, A. & Nunes, L. (2019). "Hotel Booking Demand Datasets." Data in Brief. Individual reservation-level records (lead time, cancellation, rate) — similar granularity philosophy, though 1D/tabular rather than a DTA surface. Public copy: Kaggle: Hotel booking demand.

Deep probabilistic forecasting (Phase II baselines)

  • Salinas, D., Flunkert, V. & Gasthaus, J. (2017/2019). "DeepAR: Probabilistic Forecasting with Autoregressive Recurrent Networks."
  • Lim, B., Arık, S.O., Loeff, N. & Pfister, T. (2019/2020). "Temporal Fusion Transformers for Interpretable Multi-horizon Time Series Forecasting." The interpretable-attention architecture closest in spirit to an axial-attention encoder over (arrival_date, DTA).
  • awslabs/gluonts — reference implementations of DeepAR, TFT, and other probabilistic forecasters; a natural source for baseline implementations.

Related, not independently re-verified — confirm before citing

  • Talluri, K.T. & van Ryzin, G.J. The Theory and Practice of Revenue Management. Springer. The standard revenue-management textbook; covers booking-curve modelling and demand unconstraining / censored-demand estimation directly relevant to unconstrained_demand / turned_away.
  • MIT's PODS (Passenger Origin-Destination Simulator), from Peter Belobaba's group — an airline revenue-management simulator that generates individual passenger bookings with capacity control enforced in chronological order. Conceptually the closest prior art to DeepPace-Sim's "generate bottom-up from reservations, reject in time order" design principle. Academic/research testbed, not a public code repository as far as we've found.

Note on terminology: framing (arrival_date, DTA) as an explicit 2D surface with diagonal booking_date structure — and arguing a model should exploit that geometry directly — doesn't map onto a single named method in the revenue-management literature found so far. It reads as a reframing of pickup/booking-window forecasting in image/tensor terms, closer to matrix-structured deep forecasting work (e.g. TFT) than to the classical 1D-per-date RM pickup literature.
